There is a generation of cars — roughly 1985 to 2005 — that represents a particular sweet spot in the history of the automobile. Old enough to be free of the multiplying electronics that now mediate every input. New enough to be reliable, safe by the standards of their era, and mechanically understandable.

These are the last cars a competent home mechanic could meaningfully work on.

## What makes a car analogue

An analogue car is one where the relationship between input and output is direct and interpretable. You turn the wheel; the rack moves; the wheels turn. The amount of effort in the wheel tells you something about the grip at the contact patch. The throttle is a cable connected to a butterfly valve. The gear lever is connected, through rods or cables, to the actual gearbox.

When these connections are replaced with electronic mediators — steer-by-wire, throttle-by-wire, software-filtered transmission — you lose the information they carried. The car becomes, from the driver's perspective, a closed system. Inputs go in; outputs come out; the process in between is invisible.

## The sensory argument

The strongest argument for analogue driving isn't efficiency or repairability — it's sensation. A car that communicates through its controls is one you can learn. The shimmy in the wheel before the front end pushes. The change in throttle response as grip decreases. The sound of the engine at the limit of traction.

These signals are information. They tell you where you are relative to the car's limits. Strip them out and you're driving by dashboard — numbers rather than feel.

## The window is closing

The era I'm describing is now old enough that the best examples are being preserved and priced accordingly. In another decade, the mechanical knowledge to restore them will be genuinely rare. If you've been meaning to find one worth keeping, the window is narrowing.
