The inline-six is a naturally balanced engine. Six cylinders firing in sequence, two groups of three alternating at 120° intervals, produce a power stroke every 120° of crankshaft rotation. The primary and secondary forces cancel each other. No balance shafts needed. No significant vibration at any engine speed.

It was replaced, for about two decades, by the V6. And now it's coming back.

## Why the V6 won

The V6 is shorter than an inline-six. In an era of transverse front-wheel-drive packaging — where the engine sits sideways behind the front bumper — a shorter engine fits better. Packaging won over refinement. Most of the industry moved on.

The V6 compromise: it's not naturally balanced. Most V6 configurations have an inherent secondary imbalance that requires counterbalancing. They're also harder to keep thermally even across all six cylinders, since the cylinders aren't in a uniform thermal environment.

## Why the straight-six is returning

Two forces drove the revival. First, longitudinal engine packaging came back in fashion for premium rear-wheel-drive vehicles — where the inline-six's length is irrelevant or even beneficial. Second, emissions and electrification created new packaging constraints that actually favor the inline-six's easier integration with a single turbocharger and electric assist motor.

BMW's S58, Jaguar-Land Rover's Ingenium six, and Mercedes' new M256 are all inline configurations. They're smoother, simpler to tune, and respond better to the single-turbo and mild-hybrid architectures that dominate current development.

## The lesson

Packaging determines configuration. When packaging constraints shift, the "best" configuration shifts with them. The inline-six was always the smoother, simpler engine. We're just back to circumstances where those qualities are valued.
