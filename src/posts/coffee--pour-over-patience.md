The kettle temperature matters. The grind matters. The bloom matters — that initial 30-second pour that releases trapped CO₂ and lets the coffee de-gas before you extract it. The flow rate matters. The water distribution matters.

Pour-over is the most demanding of the common brewing methods. It is also, in the right frame of mind, the most meditative.

## The bloom

Pour just enough water to wet the grounds — roughly twice the mass of the coffee. Watch the bed swell and bubble. This is carbon dioxide escaping, a sign of freshness. Older coffee blooms less. Coffee roasted within the past two weeks will dome and gas dramatically.

The bloom matters because CO₂ resists extraction. If you don't allow it to escape, it creates uneven flow and interferes with the dissolved solids you're trying to pull into the cup.

## The pours

Different recipes call for one continuous pour, two pours, or a series of smaller additions. The common logic: each pour agitates the grounds, influences turbulence, and affects the final extraction balance. More agitation tends toward higher extraction. Slower, gentler pours extract differently than fast aggressive ones.

There is no single correct approach. There are outcomes you prefer, and methods that tend to produce them. Consistency of technique is more important than technique choice.

## What you're actually learning

After fifty pour-overs, you're no longer thinking about the variables. You're reading the drawdown — how fast the water is falling through the bed — and adjusting in real time. The method becomes a sensor. You're no longer following a recipe; you're listening to the coffee.
